## 1. Why checking is your job

Picture a new drafting assistant who is fast, articulate and tireless, and who has one strange
habit. About one time in twenty, when they don't know something, they don't say so. They write
something that sounds right, in the same calm, confident voice as everything else. They are not
lying on purpose. They genuinely cannot tell the difference between what they know and what
they have just made up.

You wouldn't fire this assistant. They save you hours. But you would never sign their work
without reading it, and you would learn *where* their slips tend to happen, so you know where to
look.

That is the situation with every AI chat tool (as of 2026). Class 2 explained why: the model
predicts likely next words, so it produces text that *sounds* like a good answer, and most of
the time that is also a *correct* answer. When it isn't, nothing in the tone warns you. These
invented details are called **hallucinations**.

!!! tip "The one-sentence version"
    **Fluent is not the same as true.** The AI's job is to produce a plausible answer; your job is
    to decide whether it is a correct one.

Three facts shape everything in this class:

- **You are accountable.** A situation report, a briefing note or an email sent under your name
  is your work, whoever or whatever drafted it. "The AI said so" is not a defence.
- **Errors cluster in predictable places.** Numbers, names, dates, quotes and references are
  where hallucinations do the most damage and are easiest to miss.
- **Checking is cheaper than correcting.** Five minutes checking a SitRep is far less work than
  a correction notice after a donor has quoted the wrong figure.

## 2. A fact-checking routine

You don't need to check every word of every AI answer equally. A quick email reply to a
colleague needs less checking than a figure going to a donor. But for anything that matters,
use the same short routine every time. It works like a pre-flight checklist: boring, quick and
very effective.

### Step 1: Mark the checkable claims

Go through the answer and mark every **claim**, meaning anything that could be true or false.
Four kinds matter most:

| Claim type | Example in an AI answer | Why it's risky |
|---|---|---|
| **Numbers** | "1,200 people displaced" | Units get swapped (households vs people), totals get invented, estimates become facts |
| **Names and places** | "The Kessa health post" | Names get merged, misspelled or made up |
| **Quotes** | *"We have enough stock for a week," said the district officer.* | Quotes are paraphrased, attributed to the wrong person or invented |
| **Citations and sources** | "According to the 2025 Veloria Flood Assessment (p. 14)…" | References can look perfect and not exist |

A useful trick is to ask the AI to do the marking for you, then check the list yourself:

```text
List every number, name, date, quote and source reference in your answer
above as a table, with a column saying exactly where in my notes each
one comes from. Write "not in the notes" if it isn't there.
```

### Step 2: Trace each claim to a source

For each marked claim, find where it came from:

- **If you gave the AI the material** (field notes, a report, meeting minutes), find the claim
  in that material. Use your computer's search (Ctrl+F or Cmd+F) for the number or name.
- **If the AI got it from its general knowledge or a web search**, find the original source
  yourself. A link that the AI provides is a *starting point*, not proof: open it and check it
  says what the AI claims.
- **If you can't find a source**, treat the claim as unverified. Either remove it or label it
  clearly ("unconfirmed").

### Step 3: Check the meaning, not just the words

A figure can appear in the source and still be wrong in the answer. Watch for:

- **Changed units:** 1,200 *households* became 1,200 *people*.
- **Lost qualifiers:** "*approximately* 1,200, *partly estimated*" became "1,200".
- **Wrong scope:** a figure for one village is presented as a figure for the whole district.
- **Wrong time:** last week's figure is presented as today's.
- **Changed certainty:** "unconfirmed reports of" became "there are".

### Step 4: Check what's missing

Hallucinations add things; **omissions** quietly remove them. Compare the answer with the source
and ask: *Did it leave out a village, a need, a caveat, a dissenting view?* A SitRep that drops
the one village nobody could reach looks tidier and is less true.

### Step 5: Record what you checked

For important documents, keep a short note of what you verified and against what. It takes a
minute and it protects you and your team if a figure is questioned later.

!!! example "The routine on one page"
    1. **Mark** every number, name, date, quote and citation.
    2. **Trace** each one to a source you can open.
    3. **Compare meaning**: units, qualifiers, scope, time, certainty.
    4. **Look for gaps**: what's missing that should be there?
    5. **Record** what you checked for anything that matters.

## 3. Hallucinated citations

Citations deserve their own section, because they are the most convincing kind of
hallucination. An invented reference has everything a real one has: plausible authors, a
realistic title, a year, a journal or publisher, sometimes page numbers and a web address. It
looks *more* credible than a vague claim, and that's exactly what makes it dangerous.

Here is a (fictional) example. A colleague asked an AI tool for evidence on water-borne disease
after floods and got:

!!! failure "Looks real, isn't"
    > Studies show that diarrhoeal disease cases rise by 40–60% in the two weeks after major
    > flooding (Mensah & Clarke, 2021, *Journal of Humanitarian Health*, 14(3), pp. 211–226).

Everything about this looks right. But when the colleague searched for the article, there was
nothing: no such article, no such issue, and in this case no such journal. In real cases it's
often subtler: the journal exists, the authors work in a related field, but the article was
never written. The "40–60%" figure had no source at all.

Why does this happen? Because the model has seen millions of citations and has learned what one
*looks like*. When asked for a source, it produces text in the shape of a source. Unless the
tool is actually searching a document library or the web and showing you where it found things
(see [Class 4](c4.md) on RAG), the reference is generated the same way as any other sentence.

**How to resolve a citation:**

1. **Search for the exact title** in a library catalogue, a scholarly search engine or the
   publisher's website. Not in the AI tool.
2. **Open the source** and find the specific claim. Check the page or section if one is given.
3. **Check that it says what the AI says it says.** Real sources are often misquoted: a finding
   about one country becomes a global claim, or "may" becomes "does".
4. **If you can't find it, don't use it.** Don't keep the claim and quietly remove the reference.
   The claim came from the same place as the fake reference.

!!! warning "Tools with search are better, not perfect"
    Many enterprise tools can now search the web or your organization's documents and show links
    (as of 2026). This greatly reduces invented references, because the AI is quoting something
    it actually retrieved. But the summary of that source can still be wrong, or the linked page
    may not support the claim. Always click through for anything you will repeat.

## 4. Bias: who is missing, and who is described differently

Invented facts are the loud failure. **Bias** is the quiet one. An answer can be accurate
sentence by sentence and still be skewed in what it emphasises, who it assumes and who it
leaves out.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/bias.md"

It helps to think of three places bias shows up.

### Bias in the data

Models learn from very large collections of text, much of it from the internet. That text
over-represents some regions, languages, professions and points of view. What the model
"knows" about a capital city is far richer than what it knows about a remote district, and what
it has read about a well-documented crisis shapes how it describes a new one.

*Fictional example:* asked to "describe typical living conditions in Northern Veloria", an AI
tool gives a vivid, confident paragraph. None of it comes from Veloria. It is a blend of what the
model has read about other places that sound similar, and it quietly carries their stereotypes.

### Bias in outputs

Even when you provide the facts, the way an AI tool writes about people can differ by group. It
may:

- Describe men as "leaders" and women as "beneficiaries" in the same programme.
- Use more emotional, passive language for some communities ("suffering", "helpless") and more
  active language for others ("organizing", "responding").
- Assume job roles: when you ask for "a story about a doctor and a nurse", the doctor is a man
  and the nurse a woman.
- In summaries, give more space to the groups mentioned most, so a minority community mentioned
  once in the notes disappears.

*Fictional example:* a field note says that both the Aramu women's association and the village
council organized the evacuation of Lomi. The AI's summary credits "local leaders" with the
evacuation and mentions women only as "vulnerable groups requiring support".

### Bias in language coverage

AI tools perform best in English, and well in other widely used languages, but quality can drop
noticeably for languages that are less represented in the training data (as of 2026). Among major
world languages such as Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish, quality is
generally strong but not identical, and it can drop further for regional dialects, local
languages and specialist terms.

This matters in two ways:

- **Translations and summaries** of material in less-represented languages may lose nuance or
  make mistakes that a non-speaker can't see.
- **Responses to feedback** from affected communities, submitted in local languages, may be
  summarized less accurately than feedback in English, so those voices count for less without
  anyone noticing.

### How to test for bias

You can't see bias by reading one answer. You find it by **comparing**:

- **Swap the group.** Ask the same question with a different gender, region or community and
  compare the tone and assumptions.
- **Count who appears.** In a summary, list every group mentioned in the source and check they
  all appear in proportion.
- **Check the verbs.** Who *acts* and who is *acted upon*?
- **Test in other languages.** Have a speaker check a sample of translations, especially back
  translations of key messages.
- **Ask whose view is missing.** Then go and get it from a real source, not from the AI.

## 5. Automation bias: trusting it because it sounds sure

There is one more kind of bias, and this one is in *us*. **Automation bias** is the tendency to
trust a computer's output more than we should, especially when it looks polished and confident,
and to stop noticing when it's wrong. It is well documented in aviation, medicine and other fields
long before chatbots, and AI tools make it stronger, for three reasons:

1. **Everything sounds equally sure.** A chatbot's tone doesn't change between facts it has
   seen a thousand times and details it has invented. People use confidence as a signal of
   accuracy; with AI, that signal is broken.
2. **It's usually right.** After twenty good answers, we stop checking the twenty-first. That is
   exactly when a quiet error gets through.
3. **Checking feels like extra work.** When the AI produced a draft in ten seconds, spending ten
   minutes checking it feels slow, even though it's still far faster than writing it yourself.

Some practical counter-habits:

- **Decide the checking level before you read the answer**, based on what's at stake, not on how
  convincing the answer looks.
- **Ask the AI to show uncertainty**: *"For each figure, say whether it is stated directly, inferred
  or estimated."* This doesn't make it accurate, but it helps you see where to look.
- **Treat "I'm confident" as meaningless.** If the AI says "I am certain", that is a sentence it
  generated, not a measurement.
- **Rotate the reviewer.** A second person who didn't see the AI working often spots what the
  first person accepted.

!!! danger "Confidence is not evidence"
    An AI that says *"The total is definitely 4,350"* is no more likely to be right than one that
    says *"The total might be around 4,350."* Check the number, not the tone.

## 6. Asking the AI to check itself (and why that isn't enough)

In Class 3 you learned a useful move: ask the AI to review its own work against the source. It
works surprisingly often. A second pass can catch a changed number, a dropped village or an
unsupported claim. There are several good ways to do it:

```text
Review your summary against the original notes. List any number, name or
date in your summary that does not appear in the notes, and any
important point from the notes that your summary left out.
```

```text
Act as a sceptical editor. Identify the three claims in this draft most
likely to be wrong or misleading, and explain why.
```

```text
Compare this draft with the source document. For each paragraph, rate it
"supported", "partly supported" or "not supported", and quote the
sentence from the source that supports it.
```

So why isn't that enough?

- **The same blind spots.** The checker is the same model, with the same training data and the
  same gaps. If it didn't know something the first time, it still doesn't.
- **It can confirm its own errors.** Asked "Is this right?", models often say yes and give a
  confident justification. They can also "correct" something that was actually right.
- **It checks against what it can see.** It can only compare with the material in the
  conversation. It can't check the real world, or a source it can't access.
- **It can invent the evidence, too.** A self-check that "quotes the supporting sentence" can
  quote a sentence that isn't in the source. You still have to look.

Think of self-checking as a **spell-checker**: worth running every time, and catches a lot, but
nobody would publish a report on the strength of a spell-check alone. A human who knows the
subject and can open the sources makes the final call.

!!! tip "A better second opinion"
    If you want an AI second opinion, give it a **fresh chat** with only the source and the draft,
    and a specific checking task. A fresh chat doesn't "remember" why it wrote what it wrote, so
    it is less inclined to defend it. It's still not a substitute for checking the key facts
    yourself.

## 7. When not to use AI: a traffic-light rule

Checking reduces risk; it doesn't remove it. Some tasks are low-risk enough that AI is an easy
win. Some need care and a human in the loop. And some shouldn't involve a general AI chat tool
at all. A simple traffic-light rule helps you decide quickly.

| Light | When | Examples | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green:** go, with a normal check | Low stakes, no sensitive data, you can easily judge quality | Rewording an email, brainstorming titles, summarizing a public report, drafting an agenda, explaining a concept | Use your approved tool, read the output, fix what's wrong |
| **Amber:** proceed with care and a named human checker | Content that others will rely on; facts and figures; public communications; translation of important messages | SitReps, donor updates, briefing notes, data analysis, translating a press release | Use the full fact-checking routine; a knowledgeable person reviews before it goes out; record what you checked |
| **Red:** don't use a general AI tool, or only with explicit approval and special safeguards | Legal consequences; safety-critical decisions; personal or highly sensitive data; final decisions about people | Contract or legal advice; security or evacuation decisions; medical guidance; beneficiary eligibility; recruitment shortlists; disciplinary matters; anything with names and personal details of affected people | Use established procedures and qualified people. AI may at most help with a narrow, approved part, never the decision |

Some red-light areas deserve a word of explanation:

- **Legal matters.** AI tools can sound authoritative about law and regulations while being wrong,
  out of date or talking about the wrong jurisdiction. Your organization's own legal position,
  contracts and obligations add details a general model is unlikely to know.
- **Safety-critical decisions.** Whether a road is safe to travel, whether a site should be
  evacuated or how to treat a patient must rest on verified information and accountable people.
  A plausible but wrong answer can cost lives.
- **Personal data.** Names, contact details, health, protection cases or any information about
  identifiable people, especially vulnerable people, must only go into tools approved for that
  classification, and often not into AI at all. Class 8 covers this in detail.
- **Final decisions about people.** Who receives assistance, who is shortlisted for a job, who is
  disciplined: these decisions affect people's lives and rights and require accountability, a
  reasoned basis and a route to appeal. AI can reflect bias and can't be held accountable.
  **A human must make, and own, the decision.**

!!! warning "Amber tasks drift to red"
    A task can change colour as it grows. Summarizing public news about a flood is green. Adding
    names of affected families from a registration list turns it red. Check the light again each
    time you add data or raise the stakes.

## 8. Human in the loop

The phrase **human in the loop** comes up whenever AI is used for work that matters. It means
a person reviews and approves the AI's work at agreed points, before it has real effects.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/human-in-the-loop.md"

For everyday chat-tool use, *you* are the human in the loop, and the traffic-light rule tells you
how hard to look. As AI systems start acting on their own (Class 7), the checkpoints have to be
designed in on purpose. Three questions help:

1. **Where are the checkpoints?** Before anything leaves the team, before any irreversible action
   (sending, publishing, paying, deleting), and before any decision about a person.
2. **What does the checker actually check?** "Review the SitRep" is vague. "Confirm every figure
   against the field notes, confirm no personal names appear, confirm Dorra is listed as a gap" is
   a real check.
3. **Does the checker have what they need?** Time, the source material, the expertise, and the
   authority to say no.

A checkpoint that fails any of these three becomes a **rubber stamp**: a person who approves
without really checking. Rubber stamps are worse than useless, because they create the
*appearance* of oversight and let everyone relax.

!!! example "SitRep Assistant: where the human sits"
    In our running case study, the AI turns messy field notes into a draft SitRep. The reporting
    officer is the human in the loop. They check every figure against the notes using the
    routine in section 2, confirm that gaps and ambiguous dates are flagged, scan for bias in who
    is mentioned, and only then send the draft for clearance. The AI saves time on drafting; the
    officer's judgement is what makes the SitRep trustworthy.

## Prompt Lab

Work in pairs. Use your organization's **approved** AI chat tool. One partner drives, the other
plays **sceptical reviewer** and says "show me where that comes from" out loud. Swap after each
exercise. All material is fictional.

!!! example "The field note (fictional, same as Class 3, with a follow-up)"
    ```text
    FIELD NOTE - Aramu district, Northern Veloria - sent Thurs eve
    From: K. Osei, Veloria Relief Coalition field team

    heavy rain since the weekend, Tula river burst near Kessa bridge.
    we got to Aramu town + Lomi and Tavet villages today. could NOT reach
    Dorra, road cut after the bridge, no phone signal there either.

    approx 1,200 households displaced in total (our estimate + figures
    from village chiefs). most are staying at Aramu primary school, which
    is full - maybe 600 people there. more families in the community hall
    but we didn't manage to count them.

    water is the big problem. 2 of the 3 boreholes in Lomi are flooded
    and people are drinking from the river. Tavet health post still open
    but running low on ORS, nurse reports 14 cases of acute diarrhoea
    since Monday, mostly children under 5. no deaths reported to us.

    VRC is distributing food at the school, enough stock for about 3 days.
    next truck expected 04/05 if the Kessa road is passable. urgent: water
    purification, hygiene kits, ORS, shelter materials for the hall.

    FOLLOW-UP - Fri midday
    The Lomi women's group organized moving elderly people to the school
    on Wednesday night, together with the village council. Community hall
    count done: 43 families.
    ```

### Exercise 1 · Catch the invented detail

**Task:** Ask for a SitRep *without* any rule about missing information, then run the
fact-checking routine on it: mark every claim and trace each one to the note.

```text
Write a one-page situation report for the country director based on the
field note below. Include the total number of people displaced, the
number of disease cases, and the expected date of the next delivery.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field note here]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Is there a "total number of people displaced"? The note gives *households* plus two partial
  figures. Where did the AI's total come from?
- Did "04/05" become a definite date? Which one?
- Did "approx" and "our estimate" survive, or did estimates become facts?
- Count the claims you couldn't trace to the note. That's your hallucination count.

### Exercise 2 · The citation hunt

**Task:** Ask for evidence with references, then try to resolve every reference yourself using
a library catalogue, scholarly search or the publisher's website (not the AI tool).

```text
I'm writing a briefing on the health risks after flooding in rural areas.
Give me three key findings from published research, each with a full
reference (authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages) and a link.
```

**What to look for:**

- How many references could you find? How many said what the AI claimed?
- Did any look perfect but lead nowhere, or lead to a different article?
- If your tool searched the web and showed links, compare: were those more reliable?
- What would you do with a claim whose reference you couldn't find?

### Exercise 3 · Who got the credit?

**Task:** Use the **whole** note including the follow-up. Ask for a short summary, then check
who is described as acting and who as receiving help. Then swap a detail and compare.

```text
Summarize the field note below in 100 words for a public newsletter,
describing what happened and who responded.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field note including the follow-up]
"""
```

Then, in a new chat, paste the same note but change "The Lomi women's group" to "The Lomi youth
group" and ask again.

**What to look for:**

- Is the women's group mentioned at all? Is it described as *organizing* or as *vulnerable*?
- Does the wording change when the group changes? How?
- Which groups from the note are missing from the summary?
- Rewrite one sentence yourself to fix any bias you found.

### Exercise 4 · Self-check vs you-check

**Task:** Take the SitRep from Exercise 1. Ask the AI to check itself. Then compare its list of
problems with the one you made.

```text
Review the situation report you wrote against the original field note.
For every number, date and name in the report, quote the exact words in
the note that support it. If a claim is not supported, say "NOT IN
NOTE". Then list anything important from the note that the report left
out.
```

**What to look for:**

- Which of your problems did the AI catch? Which did it miss?
- Did it "quote" anything that isn't actually in the note? (Search the note to be sure.)
- Did it flag the unreached village (Dorra) as an omission?
- Would you now send the report? What still needs a human?

### Exercise 5 · Traffic lights for your job

**Task:** List five tasks from your own work that you might use AI for. Ask the AI to classify
them with the traffic-light rule, then decide for yourself and compare. (Describe the tasks in
general terms; don't paste real sensitive material.)

```text
Classify each task below as GREEN (low risk, normal check), AMBER (use
with care and a named human checker) or RED (don't use a general AI
tool; legal, safety-critical, personal data or a final decision about
people). For each, give a one-line reason and say what a human must check.

Tasks:
1. [task]
2. [task]
3. [task]
4. [task]
5. [task]
```

**What to look for:**

- Where did you and the AI disagree? Who was right, and why?
- Did any task turn out to be red only because of the data involved?
- For each amber task, write down *what* the human checker checks, not just "review".

## Key terms

- **Hallucination:** a confident, plausible-sounding statement from an AI that is not true or
  has no source.
- **Hallucinated citation:** a reference (author, title, journal) generated by an AI that looks
  real but doesn't exist or doesn't say what is claimed.
- **Fact-checking routine:** mark the claims, trace them to sources, compare meaning, look for
  gaps, record what you checked.
- **Omission:** an important fact, group or caveat from the source that the AI's output left out.
- **Bias:** a systematic skew in AI outputs for or against certain groups, places, languages or
  viewpoints.
- **Language coverage:** how well an AI tool performs in a given language, which depends on how
  much of that language it learned from.
- **Automation bias:** the human tendency to over-trust a computer's output, especially when it
  sounds confident.
- **Self-check:** asking the AI to review its own output; useful, but not a substitute for human
  verification.
- **Traffic-light rule:** green (go), amber (care and a named checker), red (don't use a general
  AI tool) for deciding when to use AI.
- **Human in the loop:** a person who reviews and approves AI work at agreed points before it has
  real effects.
- **Rubber stamp:** a human checkpoint that approves without really checking.
