### The case study: the SitRep Assistant

Throughout the course we work on one realistic task. A **situation report** (**SitRep**) is a
short, regular update that tells decision-makers what is happening in a crisis: how many people
are affected, what they need most, what is already being done and what is still unknown.
SitReps are built from many sources, and those sources are usually messy: field notes typed on a
phone, figures from village leaders, partial counts, ambiguous dates.

Our fictional setting is **Aramu district** in **Northern Veloria province**, where heavy rain has
caused the Tula river to flood. Field teams from the **Veloria Relief Coalition** send in notes
like this one:

```text
approx 1,200 households displaced in total (our estimate + figures
from village chiefs). most are staying at Aramu primary school, which
is full - maybe 600 people there. could NOT reach Dorra, road cut.
```

The **SitRep Assistant** is the idea of using AI to help turn notes like these into a clear,
honest situation report. In this course you will *use and judge* it through your chat tool. (In
the parallel [AI Engineering course](../engineering/index.md), technical colleagues *build* it.)
By Class 8 you will design an AI-assisted workflow from your own job.

!!! tip "Why a case study?"
    It's easy to be impressed by AI in general. It's more useful to ask whether it helps with
    one specific, real piece of work, and what could go wrong if it does. The SitRep gives us
    that concrete test in every class.

Today's question is the most basic one: when people say "AI", what do they actually mean?

## 1. What do we mean by "AI"?

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** is the broad field of making computers do tasks that normally
need human intelligence: understanding language, recognising images, making predictions,
planning, translating. It's an umbrella term, and a very wide one. The spell-checker in your
word processor, the system that flags suspicious bank transactions, the satellite analysis that
maps flooded areas, and the chat tool that drafts your email all get called "AI".

That width causes confusion. When a vendor says their product "uses AI", or a colleague says
"AI can't be trusted with numbers", they may be talking about completely different kinds of
system. So it helps to know the family tree.

- **Artificial intelligence** is the whole field.
- **Machine learning (ML)** is the branch of AI where the system learns patterns from examples,
  rather than following rules that a person wrote.
- **Deep learning** is a kind of machine learning that uses very large **neural networks**
  (explained below). Most of the progress you've heard about since around 2012 is deep learning.
- **Generative AI** is deep learning used to *create* new content, such as text, images, audio
  or code, instead of only sorting or scoring things.
- **Large language models (LLMs)** are generative AI for text. They are the engines inside chat
  tools like Copilot, ChatGPT and Claude.

Each circle sits inside the one before it: every LLM is generative AI, every generative AI
system uses deep learning, and so on. But the reverse isn't true. Most AI is *not* generative.

Here is how that looks with examples you may meet at work:

| System | Where it sits | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| A rule that routes emails with "invoice" in the subject to Finance | AI in the loosest sense, or just ordinary software | Follows a rule a person wrote |
| A spam filter | Machine learning | Learned from millions of emails labelled "spam" or "not spam" |
| A model that spots flooded areas in satellite images | Deep learning | Learned from many labelled images; outputs a map, not new content |
| A tool that translates field notes from French to English | Deep learning (and increasingly generative AI) | Produces new text in another language |
| A chat tool drafting a SitRep | Generative AI (an LLM) | Writes new text, word by word |

!!! tip "A useful habit"
    When someone says "AI", ask: *which kind, trained on what, and producing what?* A system
    that sorts things makes different mistakes from one that writes things.

## 2. Learning from examples vs programming rules

For most of computing history, if you wanted a computer to do something, a person wrote the
rules. A **program** is a precise list of instructions: *if* this, *then* do that. Programs are
excellent when the rules are clear. A payroll system, a budget spreadsheet and a travel
approval form are all ordinary programs. They do exactly what they were told, every time.

But some tasks are very hard to write rules for. Try writing down the rules for recognising a
cat in a photo. Pointy ears? Some cats have folded ears. Whiskers? So do dogs. Four legs? So does
a table. After a while you realise you *know* a cat when you see one, but you can't list the
rules.

**Machine learning** takes a different approach. Instead of writing the rules, you show the
system thousands or millions of **examples**, each with the right answer (cat, not cat), and
let it adjust itself until it gets most of them right. This adjusting is called **training**.
The result is a **model**: the trained system that you then use on new cases.

!!! example "Two ways to flag urgent field notes"
    **The rules approach.** A programmer writes: *if the note contains "cholera", "death" or
    "no water", mark it urgent.* It is predictable and easy to explain. But it misses "people
    drinking from the river" (no keyword), and it wrongly flags "no deaths reported" (contains
    "death").

    **The learning approach.** You collect 5,000 past field notes that experienced officers
    have already marked urgent or not urgent, and train a model on them. It picks up patterns no
    one wrote down, and it catches "drinking from the river". But you can't see *why* it
    flagged any note. And if the past officers tended to under-rate needs in one region, the
    model learns that bias too.

Neither approach is "better". They have different strengths:

| | Rules (a normal program) | Learning from examples (machine learning) |
|---|---|---|
| **Good for** | Clear, stable rules: calculations, forms, checks | Fuzzy patterns: language, images, judgement calls |
| **How it's made** | A person writes every rule | The system learns patterns from data |
| **When it's wrong** | You can find the faulty rule and fix it | Hard to say why; you fix it with better data or checks |
| **Predictable?** | Same input, same output, always | Usually, but it can surprise you on unusual cases |
| **Main risk** | Misses cases nobody wrote a rule for | Copies gaps and biases in its examples; confident mistakes |

The engine behind modern machine learning is the **neural network**.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/neural-networks.md"

Three consequences of "learning from examples" matter for everything in this course:

1. **A model is only as good as its examples.** If the training data rarely includes a
   language, a region or a kind of situation, the model will be weaker there.
2. **Nobody can read the rules.** The patterns are spread across billions of numbers. We judge
   a model by testing what it does, not by inspecting how it works.
3. **Models produce their best guess, always.** A rule-based program can stop and say "no rule
   matches". A trained model almost always gives *some* answer, whether or not it has good
   grounds for it. Class 2 shows how this leads chatbots to make things up.

## 3. A short history

AI isn't new. The field is about 70 years old, and it has had several waves of excitement
followed by disappointment. Knowing the outline helps you keep today's claims in proportion.

| When | What happened | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **1950s** | Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" (1950). The term "artificial intelligence" is coined at a 1956 workshop | The ambition is old; so are the over-optimistic predictions |
| **1970s–1980s** | **Expert systems**: thousands of hand-written "if… then…" rules capturing specialists' knowledge, e.g. for medical diagnosis | Worked in narrow areas but were brittle and costly to maintain. Funding dried up in "AI winters" |
| **1990s–2000s** | **Machine learning** spreads: spam filters, search ranking, recommendations. IBM's Deep Blue beats the world chess champion (1997) | The shift from writing rules to learning from data |
| **2012** | A deep neural network wins the ImageNet image-recognition contest by a wide margin | Start of the **deep learning** boom, powered by more data and faster chips |
| **2017** | Google researchers publish the **transformer** design | The architecture behind almost every modern chat model (Class 2) |
| **2018–2020** | Large language models grow rapidly in size and ability | Fluent text generation becomes possible |
| **November 2022** | **ChatGPT** is released and reaches 100 million users within about two months | Generative AI reaches the general public and the workplace |
| **2023–2026** | Models handle images and audio, "reasoning" modes appear, and **agents** that take actions with tools spread into workplace products (as of 2026) | From answering questions to doing tasks (Class 7) |

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/ai-timeline.md"

Why did things speed up so much after 2012? Three ingredients arrived together: **data** (the
internet provided enormous amounts of text and images), **computing power** (chips originally
built for video games turned out to be ideal for training neural networks) and **better designs**
(most importantly the transformer). None of this involved a machine suddenly "waking up". It
was engineering at a very large scale.

## 4. What generative AI is good at, and bad at

Generative AI is remarkable at some things and unreliable at others, and the line between the two
isn't always where you'd expect. Researchers sometimes call this a **jagged frontier**: the same
tool that writes a polished two-page briefing in seconds may fail to count the words in it
correctly.

| Usually good at | Usually weak at, or risky for |
|---|---|
| Drafting: emails, reports, talking points, first versions | Facts it wasn't given, especially specific figures, names, dates and references |
| Summarizing and restructuring text you provide | Recent events after its training (unless connected to search) |
| Rewriting for a different audience, tone or length | Exact counting and arithmetic on long lists, done "in its head" |
| Translating between major languages | Knowing when it doesn't know; it rarely says "I'm not sure" unprompted |
| Brainstorming options, questions, risks | Judgements that need local context, relationships or accountability |
| Explaining concepts in plain language | Consistency: ask twice, get two different answers |
| Pulling key points into a table | Anything where one invented detail causes real harm |

Look at the pattern. Generative AI is strongest when **you supply the material** and it
**transforms** it: summarize this, reformat that, rewrite for a donor. It is weakest when it must
**supply facts from memory**, because it produces *plausible* text, not *checked* text.

!!! warning "Fluent is not the same as true"
    A chat tool writes in confident, well-organised prose whether it's right or wrong. The
    quality of the writing tells you nothing about the accuracy of the content. This is the
    single most important habit to build in this course: **judge the facts, not the fluency.**

For the SitRep this gives us a first rule of thumb. Asking the AI to *turn these field notes
into a structured update* plays to its strengths. Asking it to *write a SitRep on the Aramu
floods* with no notes attached asks it to invent one, and it will.

## 5. Hype vs reality

AI attracts strong claims in both directions. Some say it will replace most office work within
a year; others dismiss it as "just autocomplete" with no real use. Both are wrong in useful
ways.

| You may hear… | The reality (as of 2026) |
|---|---|
| "AI understands what it's saying." | It is extremely good at producing language that fits, which often *looks* like understanding. Whether it "understands" in a human sense is debated. What isn't debated: it has no built-in way to check its statements against the world. |
| "AI is just autocomplete, so it's useless." | At heart it does predict text, but prediction at this scale can summarize, translate, draft and reason through problems usefully. "Just prediction" doesn't mean "just trivial". |
| "AI is objective because it's a machine." | It learns from human-written data and copies its patterns, including its gaps and biases. Class 6 covers this. |
| "AI will replace my job." | It changes *tasks* more than whole jobs. Drafting gets faster; checking, judgement, relationships and accountability remain human. |
| "It's connected to the internet, so it knows everything current." | Only if your tool has search switched on. Otherwise it knows only what it learned up to a cut-off date (Class 4). |
| "If it says it's sure, it's right." | Confidence in the wording tells you nothing. Models sound equally sure when they are wrong. |
| "The new version fixes all that." | Each generation improves, sometimes a lot. Hallucination and bias are reduced but not solved. |

Here are five questions to ask whenever you hear a claim about AI, whether from a vendor, a
news story or an enthusiastic colleague:

1. **Which kind of AI?** A chatbot, a classifier or a forecasting model?
2. **Tested on what?** On data like ours, in our languages, in our context?
3. **How often is it wrong, and how do we find out when it is?**
4. **Who checks the output, and who is accountable for it?**
5. **Compared to what?** Is it better than our current process, or only better than nothing?

## 6. Computational thinking: deciding where AI fits

The most useful skill in this course isn't a prompting trick. It's a way of looking at your work
called **computational thinking**. The name sounds technical, but it just means breaking a
problem down so that it's clear which parts a computer can do. You already do most of it when you
plan a project. It has four parts:

| Step | In plain words | In the SitRep |
|---|---|---|
| **Decompose** | Break the big job into small steps | "Write the SitRep" becomes: collect notes, extract figures, check them, write it up, approve, send |
| **Spot patterns** | Notice what repeats, and what is the same each time | Every week: same headings, same kinds of figures, same gaps (unreachable villages, unclear dates) |
| **Abstract** | Keep what matters, drop the detail that doesn't | The director needs affected numbers, needs, response and gaps, not every sentence of every note |
| **Write steps** | Describe the process clearly enough that someone else could follow it | "For each note: extract figures with units; mark estimates; list unknowns…" |

Once a job is broken into steps, you can ask of **each step**: who or what should do this?

- **AI (generative AI)** is a good fit for language work with fuzzy patterns: summarizing,
  drafting, rewording, pulling out key points, translating. Its output needs checking.
- **A normal program** is a good fit for anything with exact rules: adding up figures, checking
  a date format, filling a fixed template, sending an email at 9:00. It's predictable, cheap and
  exact, and a spreadsheet counts.
- **A human** is needed for judgement, accountability, relationships, anything sensitive, and
  the final check. If the step's consequences are serious, a person should own it.

Here is the SitRep process broken down this way:

| Step | Best done by | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Receive field notes from teams | Normal program (email, shared folder) | Routine; no judgement needed |
| Pull out figures, needs and locations from messy notes | **AI**, checked by a human | Fuzzy language; AI is fast at this but can misread or invent |
| Add up households across locations | **Normal program** (a spreadsheet) | Exact arithmetic; AI "in its head" can get sums wrong |
| Spot double-counting between teams and flag unclear dates | AI to flag, **human** to decide | AI can point out candidates; a person knows the villages |
| Draft the SitRep text in the standard format | **AI** | Drafting in a fixed structure is a strength |
| Check every figure against the source notes | **Human** (AI can help list figures to check) | Accountability; this is where invented numbers get caught |
| Decide what to say about sensitive issues (security, protection) | **Human** | Judgement and risk |
| Approve and send | **Human** | Someone must be accountable for what goes out |

Notice that AI doesn't take over the SitRep. It speeds up two or three steps inside a process
that people still design, check and own. That pattern (**break it down, give each step to the
right worker, verify**) comes back in every class of this course, and it's exactly how
engineers design larger AI systems too (see the [AI Map](../ai-map.md)).

!!! tip "The three-way question"
    For any task you're thinking of giving to AI, ask: *Does this step need language skill
    (AI), exact rules (a normal program) or judgement and accountability (a human)?* Many steps
    need two of the three.

## Prompt Lab

Work in pairs using your organization's **approved** AI chat tool. One partner types; the other
reads the answers critically and asks "How do we know that's true?" Swap each exercise. Answers
vary from run to run, so don't worry if yours differ from your neighbours'.

Some exercises use the fictional field note from our case study. Copy it once and keep it ready
to paste.

!!! example "The field report (fictional)"
    ```text
    FIELD NOTE - Aramu district, Northern Veloria - sent Thurs eve
    From: K. Osei, Veloria Relief Coalition field team

    heavy rain since the weekend, Tula river burst near Kessa bridge.
    we got to Aramu town + Lomi and Tavet villages today. could NOT reach
    Dorra, road cut after the bridge, no phone signal there either.

    approx 1,200 households displaced in total (our estimate + figures
    from village chiefs). most are staying at Aramu primary school, which
    is full - maybe 600 people there. more families in the community hall
    but we didn't manage to count them.

    water is the big problem. 2 of the 3 boreholes in Lomi are flooded
    and people are drinking from the river. Tavet health post still open
    but running low on ORS, nurse reports 14 cases of acute diarrhoea
    since Monday, mostly children under 5. no deaths reported to us.

    VRC is distributing food at the school, enough stock for about 3 days.
    next truck expected 04/05 if the Kessa road is passable. urgent: water
    purification, hygiene kits, ORS, shelter materials for the hall.
    ```

### Exercise 1 · Meet the tool

**Task:** Ask the AI to describe itself, then judge how much of its answer you can check.

```text
In plain language, for a colleague with no technical background:
1. What kind of AI system are you (use the terms artificial intelligence,
   machine learning, deep learning, generative AI and large language
   model)?
2. What are you good at, and what are you bad at?
3. Can you look things up on the internet right now, and how would I
   know?
Keep it under 250 words.
```

**What to look for:**

- Does its description match the family tree in Section 1? Where does it place itself?
- Does its "bad at" list include making things up, recent events and arithmetic?
- On question 3: is its answer about search consistent with what your tool actually shows (for
  example, a search indicator or links)? Models are not always accurate about their own features.
- How confident does it sound? Is that confidence earned?

### Exercise 2 · Place it on the family tree

**Task:** Ask the AI to sort some everyday systems into the family tree, then check its sorting
against Section 1 and discuss any disagreements.

```text
Place each of these systems in the most specific category that fits:
"ordinary program (rules)", "machine learning", "deep learning" or
"generative AI". Give a one-line reason for each.

1. A spreadsheet formula that adds up households across villages
2. An email spam filter
3. A tool that detects flooded areas in satellite images
4. A chatbot that drafts a situation report
5. A rule that flags any field note containing the word "cholera"
6. An app that turns a voice message into written text
7. A tool that generates a picture from a text description
```

**What to look for:**

- Does it correctly call items 1 and 5 ordinary programs, not AI?
- Items 3 and 6 are deep learning but not necessarily *generative*. Does it make that
  distinction, or call everything "AI"?
- If you disagree with it, who is right? Argue it out with your partner. There are borderline
  cases (item 6 can be built either way), and that's a good discussion.

### Exercise 3 · Strengths and weaknesses, side by side

**Task:** Send three requests in the same chat. The first plays to generative AI's strengths; the
second and third don't. Compare the results.

```text
Request A:
Summarize the field note below in five bullet points for a country
director. Use only information in the note.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field report here]
"""
```

```text
Request B:
What was the population of Aramu district at the last census, and how
many health posts does Northern Veloria province have?
```

```text
Request C:
In the field note above, count exactly how many times the letter "a"
appears, and then add up every number that appears in the note.
```

**What to look for:**

- Request A: is the summary accurate? Does it stick to the note?
- Request B: Veloria is fictional, so there is no true answer. Did the AI say it doesn't know, or
  did it produce confident figures? If it invented numbers, you have just seen a hallucination,
  which Class 2 explains.
- Request C: do you believe its counts? How would you check them? What's the better tool for
  this step (hint: Section 6)?
- Which of the three would you trust in a real SitRep without checking?

### Exercise 4 · Hype check

**Task:** Use the AI to stress-test an AI sales claim, then compare its questions with the five
questions in Section 5.

```text
A vendor tells our relief coalition: "Our AI reads every field report
and produces a 100% accurate situation report automatically, with no
need for human review. It works in any language."

Act as a sceptical but fair adviser. List the questions we should ask
the vendor before believing this claim, and explain briefly why each
question matters. Then say which parts of the claim are plausible and
which are not.
```

**What to look for:**

- Does it question "100% accurate" and "no need for human review"?
- Does it ask about languages, testing on data like ours, and who is accountable for errors?
- Which of *our* five questions did it miss? Which of its questions would you add to our list?
- Notice the irony: you're asking an AI to be sceptical about AI. Did it do a good job? Why might
  it?

### Exercise 5 · AI, program or human?

**Task:** Apply computational thinking to a real, recurring task from your own job. It must be
**safe to discuss** with your approved tool, so use general descriptions only, no confidential
details. Fill in the brackets.

```text
I work as [your role] in [type of organization]. One task I do regularly is
[describe the task in two or three sentences, without confidential
details].

Help me apply computational thinking to it:
1. Break the task into its individual steps (decompose).
2. For each step, say whether it is best done by generative AI, by a
   normal program (such as a spreadsheet or form), or by a human, and
   give a one-line reason.
3. Point out which steps need a human check even if AI does them.
Present it as a table.
```

**What to look for:**

- Is the breakdown realistic? What steps did it miss that only you would know about?
- Did it give *everything* to AI? Push back where a spreadsheet or a person is the better choice.
- Does it keep humans on judgement, sensitive issues and final approval?
- Keep this table. You'll come back to it in Class 5 and in your final project in Class 8.

## Key terms

- **Artificial intelligence (AI):** the broad field of making computers do tasks that normally
  need human intelligence.
- **Machine learning (ML):** a branch of AI where the system learns patterns from examples
  instead of following rules a person wrote.
- **Deep learning:** machine learning using large neural networks with many layers.
- **Generative AI:** AI that creates new content such as text, images, audio or code.
- **Large language model (LLM):** a generative AI model for text; the engine inside chat tools.
- **Neural network:** a model made of many simple connected units whose adjustable weights are
  tuned during training.
- **Training:** the process of adjusting a model using examples until it performs the task well.
- **Model:** the trained system that is then used on new inputs.
- **Program (rules-based system):** software that follows instructions written by a person.
- **Expert system:** an early form of AI built from many hand-written "if… then…" rules.
- **Jagged frontier:** the uneven pattern of AI ability, strong at some tasks and weak at
  similar-looking ones.
- **Computational thinking:** breaking a problem into steps (decompose, spot patterns,
  abstract, write steps) so it's clear what a computer, AI or human should do.
- **SitRep (situation report):** a short regular update for decision-makers on a crisis.
