## 1. The right mindset: you are still the author

Think of the AI as a very fast, very willing junior colleague who drafts beautifully and never
says "I'm not sure". They can save you hours. They can also hand you a polished paragraph that
contains a wrong date, a softened caveat or a figure from nowhere, and they'll never tell you
which. Anything you send, sign or publish is still **your** work. The AI changes how you produce
it, not who is responsible for it.

A useful habit before any AI-assisted task is to break it down, the way you'd plan work for a
team:

1. **What are the steps?** For example: gather the facts, check them, draft, review, send.
2. **Which steps is the AI good at?** Usually: first drafts, restructuring, shortening, changing
   tone, spotting gaps, generating options.
3. **Which steps need a human?** Usually: deciding what is true, what matters, what is sensitive,
   and what is sent in the organization's name.
4. **How will I check the result?** Decide this *before* you start, not after the draft looks
   good.

This is the same "break it down → decide who does what → verify" thinking that runs through both
courses. The rest of this class applies it to six common tasks and then to the SitRep.

!!! danger "Every task in this class starts with the same check"
    *Is my tool approved for this material?* Emails, donor information, meeting content and field
    data can all include personal or sensitive information. Anonymise where you can, and don't
    paste what the tool isn't cleared for. See the [data rules on the About page](../about.md#data).

## 2. Drafting: emails, talking points, donor updates

Drafting is where most people start, and where the time savings are clearest. The blank page is
often the slowest part of writing; the AI removes it.

**What AI does well:** first drafts, alternative versions, adjusting tone ("warmer", "more
formal", "shorter"), turning bullet points into prose and prose into bullet points.

**What goes wrong:** the draft sounds generic ("We are pleased to inform you…"), invents details
to fill gaps (a date, a partner, a figure), overstates results ("significantly improved") or uses
a tone that doesn't fit the relationship.

```text
Draft a short email from me to [recipient and their role].

Purpose: [what I need them to do or know], by [deadline].
Relationship and tone: [e.g. we work together often; warm but
professional / first contact; formal].
Key points to include, in this order:
- [point 1]
- [point 2]
- [point 3]

Rules: under 150 words; no information beyond the points above; if
something needed is missing (a date, a name), put it in [square
brackets] for me to fill in rather than inventing it.
```

The last rule is the most useful one in drafting. Square-bracket placeholders turn "the AI
invented a meeting date" into "the AI showed me where I need to add a date".

For **talking points**, tell the AI who will speak, to whom and for how long: *"Five talking
points for our country director at a donor roundtable, 90 seconds in total, each point one
sentence with one supporting fact from the notes below."* For **donor updates**, a couple of past
approved updates as examples will match your house style better than any description of it.

## 3. Summarizing long reports, and what gets lost

A 60-page evaluation, a 30-page policy, a long email thread: summarizing is one of the most
valuable things AI does. It is also where losses are hardest to see, because you haven't read the
original.

**What typically gets lost or distorted:**

- **Caveats and hedges.** "Preliminary data suggest a possible increase" becomes "cases
  increased".
- **Numbers and their units.** Households become people; "up to 400" becomes "400"; a percentage
  loses what it's a percentage of.
- **Minority views and disagreement.** A summary tends to report the majority position and drop
  the partner who objected.
- **What the document *doesn't* say.** A summary rarely tells you "the report has no data for
  Dorra".
- **Anything in the middle or the annexes**, especially in long documents (Class 4, Section 7).

So ask for a summary that exposes what it did and didn't cover:

```text
Summarize the report below for [reader], who needs to [decide / know
what].

Format:
1. Key points: 5 bullets, one sentence each.
2. Figures: a table of every key figure with its unit and the page or
   section it comes from.
3. Caveats: any limitations, uncertainties or disagreements the report
   itself mentions, in its own words.
4. Not covered: important topics a reader might expect that the report
   does not address.

Use only the report. Keep hedging words ("preliminary", "estimated",
"up to") exactly as they appear.

Report:
"""
[paste or upload the report]
"""
```

Then spot-check: pick two figures from the table and find them in the original. If either is
wrong, check them all.

## 4. Translating and multilingual work

Between widely used languages (for example Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and
Spanish), AI translation is now good enough for many everyday purposes (as of 2026): understanding an incoming
document, a first draft of routine correspondence, checking a colleague's translation. It is not
a replacement for professional translation of official documents, and your organization may have
rules about which is which.

**What goes wrong:** official terms are replaced by natural-sounding but non-standard ones;
numbers, dates and names shift; tone changes (a firm request becomes a polite suggestion, or the
reverse); formal registers differ between languages and the AI may pick the wrong one.

Three moves make AI translation safer:

1. **Give it your terminology.** A short glossary of the terms that must be translated a
   particular way (check them against your organization's terminology list or an official
   terminology database).
2. **Ask it to flag, not just translate.** Terms it wasn't sure about, idioms with no direct
   equivalent, and places where the tone had to change.
3. **Check with a back-translation or a second reader.** Ask a new chat to translate it back, then
   compare meaning, or have a colleague who reads the language review it.

```text
Translate the text below from [source language] into [target language]
for [audience], in a [formal / neutral / warm] register.

Use these fixed terms:
- [term] → [required translation]
- [term] → [required translation]

Rules:
- Keep all numbers, dates, names and place names exactly as in the
  source.
- After the translation, list: (a) any term you were unsure about,
  (b) any phrase where you changed the tone or structure, and why.

Text:
"""
[paste text]
"""
```

To **check** an existing translation rather than produce one, paste both versions and ask: *"Compare
the source and the translation sentence by sentence. List any differences in meaning, numbers,
names, tone or terminology. Don't rewrite it; just list the issues."*

## 5. Data analysis in plain words

Many enterprise tools can now read tables and spreadsheets, and some can run calculations behind
the scenes (as of 2026, often labelled "analysis" or "code interpreter"). This is useful for
questions like "Which district received the fewest kits per household?" or "What changed between
last month and this month?"

**What goes wrong:** the AI misreads a column, skips rows, adds up a range as if it were a number,
mixes units, or, if it isn't actually calculating, produces a number that *looks* computed but was
predicted like any other word (Class 2). The answer arrives in the same confident tone either
way.

Rules of thumb:

- **Tell it what the columns mean** and what the units are.
- **Ask it to show its working**: the rows it used and the calculation, not just the result.
- **Check at least one number yourself** with a calculator or your spreadsheet. If the tool offers
  a real calculation feature, prefer it for anything beyond simple arithmetic.
- **Ask for the "so what" separately** from the numbers. Interpreting a trend is a judgment; make
  sure the numbers underneath are right first.

```text
Here is a table of [what it shows]. Columns: [column] = [meaning,
unit]; [column] = [meaning, unit].

1. Restate the table's totals for each column so I can check you
   have read it correctly.
2. Answer: [your question].
3. Show your working: list the rows you used and each calculation.
4. Point out any data problems: missing values, ranges instead of
   numbers, mixed units, figures that look like errors.

Do not use any data that is not in the table.

Table:
"""
[paste table]
"""
```

Step 1, asking it to restate the totals, is a cheap check. If the totals it reads don't match
yours, stop there; everything after will be wrong.

## 6. Meeting notes

AI can turn rough notes or a transcript into a clean record with decisions and action points in a
few seconds. Some tools can record and transcribe meetings directly.

**Before you start:** if you plan to record or transcribe, everyone in the meeting must know and
agree, following your organization's rules. Sensitive discussions (staff matters, security,
negotiations) may not be suitable for AI tools at all.

**What goes wrong:** actions are assigned to the wrong person; tentative ideas become decisions
("we could try the northern route" → "the team will use the northern route"); disagreement is
smoothed over; speakers are misidentified in transcripts.

```text
Turn my rough notes below into meeting minutes.

Format:
- Meeting, date, participants (as listed in my notes only)
- Decisions: only things clearly agreed
- Action points: table with Action | Owner | Deadline. If the owner or
  deadline is not in my notes, write "not stated".
- Open questions / disagreements: anything discussed but not settled

Rules: do not turn suggestions into decisions. If you are unsure
whether something was agreed, put it under open questions.

Notes:
"""
[paste notes]
"""
```

Send the draft minutes to participants for correction before they become the record. That review
step is where misattributed actions get caught.

## 7. Brainstorming and critiquing your own drafts

Two of the most underrated uses of AI don't involve producing final text at all.

**Brainstorming.** The AI is good at generating many options quickly: titles, angles for a
campaign, risks you haven't considered, questions a donor might ask. Quantity is its strength;
you supply the judgment about which ideas are good.

```text
I am [task and context]. Give me 10 [ideas / risks / questions],
as varied as possible, one line each. Then mark the three you think
are strongest and explain why in one sentence each.
```

**Critiquing your own drafts.** Ask the AI to be the tough reviewer your draft will eventually
meet:

```text
Act as a critical reviewer: [e.g. a donor's programme officer / our
head of office / a reader with no background]. Review my draft below.

List, in order of importance:
1. Claims that are not supported by evidence in the draft
2. Places where the reader will be confused or need more context
3. Anything that could be read as overstated or insensitive
4. The single change that would most improve it

Do not rewrite the draft. Just give me the list.

Draft:
"""
[paste your draft]
"""
```

"Do not rewrite" keeps the thinking with you. The AI points; you decide what to fix and how. This
is often more useful than asking it to "improve" the text, which tends to flatten your voice into
generic prose.

## 8. The SitRep workflow, start to finish

Now put it all together on the running case study. A **situation report (SitRep)** turns messy
field information into a short, structured, reliable update for decision-makers. It's a good test
of AI at work because it needs speed *and* accuracy.

Here's the workflow, broken down by who does what:

| Step | Who | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Prepare | **Human** | Collect the field notes, remove names and sensitive details, confirm the tool is approved for this material |
| 2. Extract | AI | Pull every fact into a table with sources, units and confidence |
| 3. Check facts | **Human** | Compare the table with the notes; fix errors; decide on conflicting figures |
| 4. Draft | AI | Write the SitRep from the *checked* table, in the house format |
| 5. Self-review | AI | Compare the draft with the checked table; list anything added, changed or missing |
| 6. Review and sign off | **Human** | Read the draft as the reader will; check tone and sensitivity; approve |

Notice the design: the AI drafts **from the checked table, not from the raw notes**. Once you've
fixed the facts in step 3, the draft can only be as wrong as the table, and step 5 catches drift.

**Step 2 · Extract.**

```text
You are helping prepare a humanitarian situation report. From the
field notes below, extract every fact into a table:
Topic | Fact | Figure and unit | Location | Date | Source | Count,
estimate or second-hand?

Rules: use only the notes; write "not stated" for anything missing;
keep households and people separate; flag any ambiguous date.
After the table, list the gaps a SitRep reader would want filled.

Field notes:
"""
[paste anonymised notes]
"""
```

**Step 3 · Check facts (you).** Go down the table with the notes beside it. Correct anything
wrong in the chat: *"Row 4: the source says 14 cases, not 41. Row 7: '04/05' is 4 May, confirmed
by phone."*

**Step 4 · Draft.**

```text
Using ONLY the corrected table above, write the SitRep in this format:
Headline (one line) / Situation overview / Affected population /
Priority needs / Response so far / Gaps and unknowns.
Under 300 words. Keep every "estimate" and "not stated" label visible
in the text. Reader: the country director and partner organizations.
```

**Step 5 · Self-review.**

```text
Compare your SitRep with the corrected table, line by line. List:
1. Any figure, name, date or claim in the SitRep that is not in the
   table
2. Any label ("estimate", "not stated") that was dropped
3. Any important row in the table that the SitRep leaves out
Do not rewrite the SitRep.
```

**Step 6 · Review and sign off (you).** Read it as the country director would. Is the most urgent
need at the top? Is anything worded in a way that could cause harm if forwarded (locations of
vulnerable groups, blame on a named partner)? Would you put your name on it? Only then does it
go out.

!!! tip "Save it as a project"
    If your tool supports projects, store the SitRep format, two approved past SitReps and the
    standing rules ("use only the notes", "not stated", "keep units") there. Next week, steps 2–5
    start from the same brief for everyone on the team (Class 4, Section 3).

## 9. What to delegate, what to keep

Not every task, or every part of a task, should go to AI. Use this table as a starting point, and
adjust it to your organization's rules.

| Task or step | Delegate to AI? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| First draft of routine email, update or talking points | **Yes**, then edit | Saves the most time; low risk once you check facts and tone |
| Changing length, tone, format or structure of your own text | **Yes** | Low risk; the facts are already yours |
| Summarizing a long document for your own understanding | **Yes**, with spot-checks | Good for orientation; check before quoting it |
| Brainstorming options, risks or questions | **Yes** | Quantity is its strength; you choose |
| Critiquing your draft | **Yes** | A second pair of eyes; you decide what to change |
| Translating for understanding or a first draft | **Yes**, with checks | Check terms, numbers and names; official texts go to professional translators |
| Calculations and data analysis | **Partly** | Let it help, but verify numbers yourself or with a real calculation tool |
| Deciding which figure is correct when sources conflict | **Keep** | A judgment that needs context, contacts and accountability |
| Final wording of anything published or sent in the organization's name | **Keep** | You are accountable; check tone, sensitivity and politics |
| Decisions about people: hiring, performance, eligibility for assistance | **Keep** | High stakes, risk of bias, and often personal data |
| Anything involving data the tool isn't approved for | **Keep** (don't paste it) | Data protection comes before convenience |
| Sensitive negotiations, security information, protection cases | **Keep** | Risk of harm if leaked or mishandled; usually not approved for AI tools |

A quick test for anything not on the list: **"If the AI gets this wrong and I don't notice, how
bad is it?"** If the answer is "mildly embarrassing", delegate and check. If it's "someone could be
harmed, misled or unfairly treated", keep it, or delegate only a small, checkable part.

!!! example "Field note (fictional)"

    From: field team, Veloria Relief Coalition

    heavy rain since the weekend, Tula river burst near Kessa bridge.

### Exercise 1 · A donor email with honest placeholders

**Task:** Draft a short update email to a donor from the field note, using square-bracket
placeholders instead of invented details.

```text
Draft an email from me (programme officer, Veloria Relief Coalition)
to our contact at the Northern Fund, a regular donor we work with
closely.

Purpose: a brief update on the Aramu floods and a heads-up that we
may request a reallocation for water purification.
Tone: warm, professional, factual.
Length: under 180 words.

Use only the field note below. For anything the email needs that is
not in the note (a date, an amount, a name), insert a placeholder in
[square brackets] instead of inventing it. Do not name individual
staff.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field note]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Did it use placeholders (e.g. "[amount]", "[date]") rather than inventing a figure for the
  reallocation?
- Did "approx 1,200 households" stay an estimate, or become a fact?
- Is the tone right for a donor you know well? Ask for a more formal version and compare.

### Exercise 2 · Summarize, then find what was lost

**Task:** Ask for a very short summary, then ask the AI to audit its own summary against the
source.

```text
Summarize this field note in two sentences for a senior manager.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field note]
"""
```

Then, in the same chat:

```text
Now compare your two-sentence summary with the original note. List
every caveat, estimate label, uncertainty, gap or figure that the
summary dropped or changed. For each, say whether a senior manager
would need to know it.
```

**What to look for:**

- Which caveats disappeared: "approx", "our estimate", the uncounted community hall, Dorra not
  reached, the ambiguous "04/05"?
- Did the short summary turn "maybe 600 people" into a firm number, or mix households and
  people?
- Now use the summary prompt from Section 3 instead. Does its "Caveats" and "Not covered"
  section keep what the two-sentence version lost?

### Exercise 3 · Checking a translation

**Task:** Translate the needs section of the field note into French (or another language one
of you reads), then check the translation with a second chat.

```text
Translate the text below from English into French for a partner
agency's coordination meeting, neutral professional register.

Fixed terms:
- oral rehydration salts (ORS) → sels de réhydratation orale (SRO)
- hygiene kits → kits d'hygiène
- household → ménage

Keep all numbers, names and place names exactly as in the source.
After the translation, list any term you were unsure about.

Text:
"""
water is the big problem. 2 of the 3 boreholes in Lomi are flooded
and people are drinking from the river. Tavet health post still open
but running low on ORS, nurse reports 14 cases of acute diarrhoea
since Monday, mostly children under 5. urgent: water purification,
hygiene kits, ORS, shelter materials for the hall.
"""
```

Then open a **new chat** and paste both the English source and the French translation:

```text
Compare the English source and the French translation below sentence
by sentence. List any differences in meaning, numbers, names, tone or
terminology. Do not rewrite the translation.
```

**What to look for:**

- Were the fixed terms used every time (SRO, ménage, kits d'hygiène)?
- Did "14 cases", "2 of the 3" and "under 5" survive unchanged?
- Did the translation tidy up the note's informal style? Is that acceptable for this audience?
- If one of you reads French well: do you agree with the checker, or did it miss something?

### Exercise 4 · Analysing the table, and checking the numbers

**Task:** Ask a simple analysis question and verify the result yourself.

```text
The table below shows households and distributions by location in
Aramu district. Households are estimates.

1. Restate the total for each numeric column so I can check you have
   read it correctly.
2. For each location, calculate hygiene kits per household and water
   purification sachets per household.
3. Which visited location is least covered for hygiene kits?
4. Show your working, and point out any data problems.

Use only the table.

Table:
"""
[paste the distribution table]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Check the totals yourself: do 640 + 330 + 230 make 1,200, and 410 + 120 + 200 make 730? Did the
  AI check this, or just copy the TOTAL row?
- Check Lomi's kits per household with a calculator (120 ÷ 330 ≈ 0.36). Is the AI's figure right?
- Did it handle Dorra sensibly ("not visited" is not zero households) instead of dividing by zero
  or treating Dorra as fully covered?
- Did it mention that the household figures are estimates, so the ratios are approximate?

- In step 3, did you find anything to correct in the table? Even one error justifies the step.

- At sign-off: would you send this to a country director? What did you change by hand, and how

## Key terms

- **Delegate vs keep:** deciding which steps of a task AI can do (with checking) and which must
  stay with a person.
- **Placeholder:** a marker in [square brackets] where information is missing, used instead of
  letting the AI invent it.
- **Back-translation:** translating a translation back into the original language to check that
  the meaning survived.
- **Terminology list (glossary):** fixed translations for key terms that the AI must use.
- **Situation report (SitRep):** a short, structured update on a crisis for decision-makers.
- **Self-review:** asking the AI to compare its output with the source and list differences,
  without rewriting.
- **Human sign-off:** the final review and approval by an accountable person before anything is
  sent or published.
