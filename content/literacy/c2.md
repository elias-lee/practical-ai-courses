## 1. Autocomplete, on a grand scale

You've seen autocomplete on your phone. Type *"See you"* and it suggests *"tomorrow"*,
*"soon"* or *"later"*. It has seen enough messages to know which words usually come next.

A chatbot works on the same basic idea, taken to an extraordinary scale. At its core, a
**large language model (LLM)** does one thing: **given some text, it predicts what comes next.**
Then it adds that piece to the text and predicts again, and again, one small piece at a time,
until the answer is complete.

Suppose the model is continuing this sentence:

> *"After the floods, the most urgent need in Lomi village is clean…"*

It doesn't look the answer up. It calculates how likely each possible next word is, based on
patterns learned from an enormous amount of text. Something like:

| Possible next word | Likelihood (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| water | 81% |
| drinking | 9% |
| shelter | 3% |
| sanitation | 2% |
| everything else | 5% |

It picks one (usually a likely one, as we'll see in Section 7), adds it, and moves on to
predicting the word after that.

That's the whole trick. There is no separate "facts" department, no database of true
statements it checks against, and no plan for the whole paragraph written in advance. Everything
a chatbot does, from summarizing your notes to drafting an email to answering a question,
comes out of this repeated prediction.

!!! tip "The one-sentence version"
    **A chatbot is a very sophisticated next-word predictor.** It produces the text that is most
    *plausible* given what it has been given, and plausible is not the same as true.

"Just predicting the next word" may sound too simple to explain what these tools can do. The
surprise of the last few years is how far it goes. To predict the next word in a legal
argument, a translation or a worked maths problem *well*, a model has to capture a great deal
about grammar, facts, reasoning and style. Prediction at this scale can look a lot like
understanding. But the mechanism explains the tools' weaknesses, and that's what this class is
about.

## 2. Tokens: the pieces the model reads

We've been saying "next word", but models actually work with **tokens**. A token is a chunk of
text: often a whole common word, sometimes part of a word, sometimes a space or punctuation. The
word "water" might be one token; "purification" might be split into two or three.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/tokens.md"

Why does this matter to you? Three reasons.

- **Limits are counted in tokens.** How much text a model can handle at once, and how long its
  answer can be, are measured in tokens, not pages.
- **Cost is counted in tokens.** When your organization pays for AI use, it usually pays per
  token, both for what you send and what comes back.
- **Some odd failures come from tokens.** Ask a chatbot how many letters are in a long word, or
  to spell a word backwards, and it may get it wrong. It never saw the individual letters, only
  the chunks. (Newer models handle this better, but it's a good reminder of how they see text.)

## 3. Where the patterns come from: training

A chat model is made in stages, a bit like the training of a new colleague.

**Stage 1: Pre-training.** The model reads an enormous amount of text (a large share of the
public web, books, articles, code) and practises one task billions of times: predict the next
token, check against what actually came next, adjust slightly. The adjusting happens in the
model's **parameters**, billions of numbers inside its neural network (see the Class 1 sidebar on
neural networks). After weeks of this on thousands of specialised chips, the result is a **base
model**. It has absorbed a vast amount of language, facts and patterns, but it isn't a helpful
assistant yet. Ask it a question and it might continue with three more questions, because
that's what often follows a question on a web page.

**Stage 2: Fine-tuning.** The base model is trained further on a much smaller, carefully written
set of example conversations: a question and a good, helpful answer. Now it learns to *respond*
like an assistant.

**Stage 3: Learning from human feedback.** People (and increasingly AI graders following
written guidelines) compare pairs of answers and pick the better one. The model is adjusted to
produce more answers like the preferred ones. This shapes its tone, its helpfulness, and what it
refuses to do.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/training-stages.md"

Two things follow from this, and they are worth remembering:

1. **The model doesn't learn from your chats as you use it.** Training happens before release.
   When you correct it in a conversation, it adjusts within that conversation only. (Whether
   your organization's conversations are ever used for future training is a question about your
   tool's contract and settings. Enterprise tools usually exclude it, but check. Class 8 covers
   this.)
2. **The feedback stage rewards answers that people like.** People tend to prefer answers that
   are confident, complete and agreeable. That's partly why chatbots rarely say "I don't know"
   and sometimes agree with you too readily.

## 4. The transformer and attention

How does the model decide what comes next, given everything before it? Almost every modern chat
model uses a design called the **transformer**, introduced by Google researchers in 2017. (It's
the "T" in "GPT", which stands for *generative pre-trained transformer*.)

Its key idea is **attention**. When the model processes each token, it looks at *all* the other
tokens in the text and works out which ones are most relevant to it. That's how it can keep track
of what "it" refers to, twenty words later, or tell that "bank" in a sentence about interest
rates means a financial bank.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/transformer.md"

Before any of this, each token is turned into a long list of numbers that captures something of
its meaning, called an **embedding**. Words with similar meanings get similar numbers. Attention
then works on these lists of numbers, layer after layer, gradually building a richer picture of
what the whole text means, until the final layer produces the likelihoods for the next token.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/embeddings.md"

You don't need to remember the mechanics. The practical point is this: **attention works on the
text in front of the model.** The model is constantly asking "which parts of *this* text matter
for what comes next?" If the important information isn't in the text, attention can't find it.

## 5. The context window: what the model can see

Everything the model can pay attention to at one moment is called its **context**, and the
maximum amount it can handle is its **context window**, measured in tokens.

In a chat tool, the context usually includes:

- hidden instructions from the tool provider or your organization (a **system prompt**, covered
  in Class 3);
- the whole conversation so far: your messages *and* its replies;
- any documents or files you've attached, or search results the tool has pulled in.

Every time you send a message, the whole context is sent to the model again, and it predicts its
reply from all of it. That's why it can refer back to something you said ten messages ago in the
same chat, but knows nothing about a chat you had yesterday.

!!! example "A desk, not a filing cabinet"
    Think of the context window as the model's **desk**. Whatever is on the desk it can read and
    use. Its training is more like a **vague memory** of everything it once read, useful but
    blurry and out of date. It has no filing cabinet where it can look up your documents unless
    your tool puts them on the desk.

Context windows have grown enormously. Many models can now take hundreds of thousands of tokens,
enough for several long reports at once (as of 2026). But bigger isn't magic:

- **When the desk is full, something falls off.** In a very long conversation, tools may drop or
  summarize the earliest messages. The model may then "forget" instructions you gave at the
  start.
- **Long contexts get patchy.** Models tend to pay most attention to the beginning and end of a
  long text and can miss details in the middle.
- **More text costs more** time and money, because every token is processed.

Class 4 is all about putting the *right* information on the desk.

## 6. Why chatbots make things up

You may have heard the term **hallucination**: a chatbot stating something false as if it were
fact. A made-up statistic, a report that doesn't exist, a quotation nobody said, a reference with
a real-sounding title and invented authors. It is the most important weakness to understand,
and now you have everything you need to see why it happens.

1. **It predicts plausible text, not true text.** If the most plausible-sounding continuation of
   *"The population of Aramu district is…"* is a number, the model will produce a number,
   whether or not it has ever seen one. Veloria is fictional, so any number it gives is invented.
2. **It has no built-in fact check.** Nothing inside the model compares its sentence with a
   trusted source before writing it (unless your tool adds a search or document step, and even
   then it can misread).
3. **Its memory of training is blurry.** It absorbed patterns, not a neat archive. Rare facts,
   specific figures, exact dates and names are exactly where the blur is worst.
4. **Training rewarded answering.** Across its training, a confident, complete answer was
   usually rewarded more than "I don't know". So it tends to fill gaps rather than admit them.
5. **You may have asked for something that doesn't exist.** Ask for "the total number of
   displaced people" when your notes only give households, and it may helpfully produce one.

!!! danger "Hallucinations look exactly like facts"
    There is no warning light. An invented figure is written in the same fluent, confident style
    as a correct one. The only defence is to **check**: anything specific (numbers, names,
    dates, quotations, references) that will be used in your work must be verified against a
    real source. Class 6 covers verification habits in depth.

What reduces hallucination (but never removes it)?

- **Give it the source.** Paste the field notes; don't ask it to recall facts. Summarizing text
  it can see is far more reliable than recalling from memory.
- **Tell it what to do when information is missing.** *"If it isn't in the notes, write 'not
  stated'."* (Class 3.)
- **Ask it to point to where each figure came from**, then spot-check.
- **Use tools that ground answers in documents or search**, with citations you can click
  (Class 4).

## 7. Why answers vary

Ask the same question twice and you'll often get two different answers: different wording,
sometimes different structure, occasionally a different conclusion. This surprises people who
expect computers to be consistent.

Go back to the table in Section 1. At each step the model has a list of possible next words with
likelihoods. It doesn't always pick the top one. Most tools pick with a little **randomness**,
like rolling weighted dice: "water" comes up most often, but sometimes "drinking" does, and from
that point the rest of the sentence goes a different way.

How much randomness is controlled by a setting called **temperature**.

- **Low temperature:** the model almost always picks the most likely word. Answers are more
  predictable and repetitive.
- **High temperature:** less likely words get picked more often. Answers are more varied and
  creative, and more likely to drift or go wrong.

In most enterprise chat tools you can't set the temperature directly; the provider chooses a
middle setting. (Some tools offer "more creative / more precise" styles, which have a similar
effect.) Even at the lowest setting, answers can still vary slightly.

What this means for you:

- **One answer is a sample, not *the* answer.** If a result matters, try it twice. If the two
  answers disagree on a fact, that's a strong sign the model is unsure, and you should check.
- **Variation is useful for creative work.** Ask for three options; you'll get genuinely
  different ones.
- **Variation is a problem for repeatable work.** For a weekly SitRep, you want consistency.
  That comes from a clear template and format rules (Class 3), not from hoping the model says
  the same thing twice.

## 8. What the model knows, and when it stopped learning

Because pre-training happens before release, a model's built-in knowledge stops at a
**knowledge cutoff** date. It doesn't know about events after that date unless your tool gives it
search results or documents. Ask about something recent without search and it may say it doesn't
know, or it may confidently describe an out-of-date situation. Class 4 covers knowledge cutoff
and how tools work around it.

## 9. Not all models are the same

"The AI" isn't one thing. Behind your chat tool there is at least one model, often several, and
you'll hear several distinctions as you read about AI. Here are the four that matter most for
non-technical colleagues (as of 2026).

**Reasoning models** "think before answering". They produce a long internal working-out before
their reply, which helps with multi-step problems like reconciling figures from several teams.
They're slower and cost more.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/reasoning-models.md"

**Multimodal models** work with more than text: photos, scanned documents, charts, audio. You can
give them a photo of a handwritten list or a voice note.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/multimodal.md"

**Open vs closed models.** Some models can only be used through their maker's service; others
have their trained weights published so organizations can run them on their own computers.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/open-vs-closed.md"

**Small vs large models.** Bigger models are generally more capable; smaller ones are faster,
cheaper and can run locally.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/small-vs-large.md"

| If you need… | Consider… |
|---|---|
| A careful multi-step analysis | A reasoning mode, if your tool offers it |
| To work from a photo, scan or recording | A multimodal tool, if it's approved for that data |
| Data to stay inside your organization | An approved internally hosted model (often open-weight) |
| A quick rewrite or simple sorting task | A smaller, faster model is often enough |

You usually don't choose these yourself. Your organization chooses which tools and models are
approved. But knowing the differences helps you understand why two tools behave differently,
and ask good questions when a new one is proposed.

## 10. Languages: why tokens cost more in some

Many organizations work in several languages. Take six widely used ones: **Arabic, Chinese,
English, French, Russian and Spanish**. Chat models can work in all six, but not always equally
well, and not at the same cost.

Remember that tokenizers chop text into chunks learned mostly from English-heavy data. Common
English words tend to be single tokens. Words in other languages, especially those with
different scripts or rich word endings, are often split into more pieces. So **the same sentence
can need noticeably more tokens in Arabic or Russian than in English**, sometimes one and a half
to two times as many or more, depending on the model's tokenizer. Chinese characters are counted
differently again. Newer tokenizers have narrowed the gap, but it hasn't disappeared (as of
2026).

That has practical effects:

- **Cost:** the same SitRep may cost more to process in one language than another.
- **Limits:** a context window holds fewer pages of text in a language that needs more tokens.
- **Quality:** languages that were less common in the training data may get weaker answers:
  less fluent phrasing, more errors in names and figures, or English turns of phrase slipping
  in. Major world languages like these six are generally well supported, but many smaller and
  local languages often are not.

!!! tip "In practice"
    If you work across languages, check important output in each language with a fluent speaker.
    Don't assume that because the English answer was good, the Arabic or Russian one is equally
    good.

## Prompt Lab

Work in pairs using your organization's **approved** AI chat tool. Today's exercises are
**explorations**: you're not trying to get a good SitRep, you're trying to see how the machine
behaves, including how it breaks. One partner types; the other keeps notes on anything
surprising. Swap each exercise.

### Exercise 1: Be the model: predict the next word

**Task:** First, *without* the AI, each partner writes down the next three words of the sentence
below. Compare. Then ask the AI.

```text
Continue this sentence with exactly the next three words, and then list
five other three-word continuations you considered, from most to least
likely:

"After the floods, the most urgent need in Lomi village is"
```

**What to look for:**

- Did you and your partner predict similar words? Why? (You've both read a lot of similar
  text; so has the model.)
- Are the model's alternatives all *plausible*? Are any of them *true*? It has no way of knowing
  what Lomi actually needs.
- Note: the model can't truly report its own internal likelihoods; its "list of alternatives" is
  itself a prediction. It's an illustration of the idea, not a window into the machine.

### Exercise 2: Same question, different answers

**Task:** Send the prompt below. Then open a **new chat** and send exactly the same prompt. Do it a
third time if you have time. Compare the answers.

```text
Write a two-sentence headline summary for a situation report about
flooding in Aramu district, Northern Veloria, where about 1,200
households have been displaced and clean water is the most urgent need.
```

**What to look for:**

- How different are the three versions in wording? In structure?
- Did any version add a fact that wasn't in the prompt (a number of people, a date, an
  organization)? Did different versions add *different* invented facts? That's variation and
  hallucination together.
- If you needed the same format every week, what would you add to the prompt? (Class 3 will
  answer this.)

### Exercise 3: Break the model

**Task:** This is an open exploration. Your goal is to find where the model fails. Try the prompts
below one at a time (new chat for each), then invent your own. Keep a list of what broke and
*why* you think it broke, using the ideas from this class.

```text
1. How many times does the letter "r" appear in "strawberry refrigerator"?
   Then write "humanitarian" backwards.

2. List three published reports about the 2025 Tula river floods in
   Northern Veloria, with authors, titles and dates.

3. What is 48,317 multiplied by 7,926? Answer with just the number.

4. A field team visited Lomi and Tavet. Another team visited Tavet and
   Dorra. The first counted 400 households, the second 250. How many
   households are there in total?

5. Is it true that the Veloria Relief Coalition won an award in 2024?
   Just answer yes or no.
```

**What to look for:**

- **Prompt 1:** the answers are 7 and "nairatinamuh". Mistakes here come from tokens: the model
  sees chunks, not letters.
- **Prompt 2:** Veloria is fictional. Did it invent reports with realistic authors and titles, or
  say it could find none? This is the classic hallucinated reference.
- **Prompt 3:** check with a calculator (the answer is 382,960,542). Exact arithmetic is a job
  for a normal program. Did a reasoning mode, if you have one, do better?
- **Prompt 4:** did it just add 400 + 250, or notice that Tavet may be double-counted and the
  total can't be known from this? This is a real SitRep trap.
- **Prompt 5:** a yes/no question with no basis for an answer. Did it guess? Did it say it
  couldn't know? Ask again in a new chat. Do you get the same answer?
- **Your own:** try a trick question, a question with a false assumption built in ("Why did
  the Veloria Relief Coalition close its Aramu office in 2021?") or a request in another language. Bring your best
  "break" to the debrief.

### Exercise 4: What's on the desk?

**Task:** Test the context window. In one chat, give the AI an instruction and a fact, have a
short unrelated exchange, then check whether it still uses them. Then open a **new chat** and ask
again.

```text
For this conversation: always end your replies with the word "ARAMU" in
capitals. Also, remember that the next food truck is due on Tuesday.
Reply "OK" to confirm.
```

Then send two or three unrelated questions (for example, "Suggest three names for a hygiene
promotion campaign"), and finally:

```text
When is the next food truck due?
```

Now open a **new chat** and ask only: *"When is the next food truck due?"*

**What to look for:**

- In the first chat, did it keep ending with "ARAMU"? Did it remember Tuesday? Both are on its
  desk.
- In the new chat, did it say it doesn't know, or did it guess a day? Nothing from the first chat
  is on the new desk (unless your tool has a memory feature switched on).
- What does this tell you about repeating key context when you start a new chat?

### Exercise 5: Six languages

**Task:** Translate the same SitRep headline into all six languages from Section 10, then compare.

```text
Translate this situation report headline into Arabic, Chinese, French,
Russian and Spanish. Then, for each language, tell me roughly how many
tokens you think the translation uses compared with the English, and
explain why the numbers differ.

"About 1,200 households displaced in Aramu district; clean water and
oral rehydration salts are the most urgent needs."
```

**What to look for:**

- If anyone in your group reads one of the languages, check the translation. Are names (Aramu),
  numbers and technical terms such as "oral rehydration salts" handled correctly?
- Does the model's explanation of token differences match Section 10?
- Can the model count its own tokens accurately? Usually not. It's *predicting* an answer about
  itself. If your facilitator shows a tokenizer tool, compare the real counts.
- What would you check before sending an AI translation of a SitRep to partners?

## Key terms

- **Large language model (LLM):** a model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next
  token; the engine of chat tools.
- **Next-token prediction:** the core mechanism: given text, predict the most plausible next
  piece, add it, repeat.
- **Token:** a chunk of text (a word, part of a word or punctuation) that a model reads and
  writes; limits and costs are counted in tokens.
- **Tokenizer:** the component that splits text into tokens.
- **Parameters (weights):** the billions of numbers inside a model that are adjusted in training.
- **Pre-training:** the first, largest training stage, in which the model learns to predict text
  from an enormous collection of documents.
- **Base model:** a pre-trained model before it has been tuned to act as an assistant.
- **Fine-tuning:** further training on a smaller, targeted set of examples.
- **RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback):** adjusting a model towards answers that
  people rate as better.
- **Transformer:** the model design behind almost all modern LLMs.
- **Attention:** the transformer's way of working out which parts of the text matter most for
  each token.
- **Embedding:** a list of numbers representing the meaning of a piece of text.
- **Context / context window:** the text the model can see at once, and its maximum size in
  tokens.
- **Hallucination:** a confident statement from an AI that is false or invented.
- **Temperature:** a setting that controls how much randomness goes into choosing each next token.
- **Knowledge cutoff:** the date after which a model has no built-in knowledge.
- **Reasoning model:** a model trained to work through a problem internally before answering.
- **Multimodal model:** a model that handles more than one kind of input or output, such as
  text, images and audio.
- **Open-weight model:** a model whose trained weights are published so others can run it.
- **Closed (proprietary) model:** a model available only through its maker's service.
- **Small language model:** a model with relatively few parameters, faster and cheaper to run.
