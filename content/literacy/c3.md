## 1. Why prompts matter

Imagine a very capable new colleague on their first morning. They have read a huge amount, they
write quickly, they never get tired and they are keen to help. But they have never been to your
office. They don't know your programme, your donors, your acronyms, who reads your reports, or
what "the usual format" means. They also won't ask many questions. Give them a vague request and
they will do their best, with confidence, and fill the gaps with sensible-sounding guesses.

That is roughly what you are working with every time you open an AI chat tool.

If you say to this new colleague *"Write something about the floods"*, you will get *something*.
It might be a news article, a poem or a history of flooding. It will probably be generic, too
long and pitched at the wrong reader. That isn't their fault. You didn't tell them what you
needed.

Now imagine you say: *"You're helping me prepare the weekly situation report for our country
director. Here are this morning's field notes from Aramu district. Pull out the number of people
affected, the main needs and what's already being done, in five bullet points of one line each.
If a figure isn't in the notes, write 'not stated'. Don't guess."* Same colleague, very
different result.

A **prompt** is simply everything you type into the AI tool: your question, your instructions,
any text you paste in, and your follow-up messages. **Prompting** is the skill of writing those
instructions so the AI produces something useful. It isn't programming and it isn't a secret
code. It is the same skill as briefing a person well, and most professionals already have it.
You write terms of reference, brief consultants and give handover notes. This class shows you
how to use that skill with AI.

!!! tip "The one-sentence version"
    **The AI can only use what you give it.** Everything it doesn't know about your situation it
    will guess, and it guesses in fluent, confident prose.

Remember from Class 2 that the model works by predicting likely next words given the text in
front of it. A short, vague prompt gives it very little to go on, so it falls back on the most
*average* answer, like a generic essay anyone might have asked for. A detailed prompt pulls the
prediction towards *your* situation.

## 2. The anatomy of a good prompt

Good prompts tend to contain the same six building blocks. You won't need all six every time.
A quick question needs one or two. But when an answer disappoints you, the missing block is
nearly always on this list.

| Block | What it tells the AI | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| **Role** | Who it should act as, which sets expertise, vocabulary and viewpoint | *"You are an experienced humanitarian reporting officer."* |
| **Task** | What exactly you want done, using a clear action verb | *"Summarize the field notes below into a situation update."* |
| **Context** | The background: who it's for, why, what's already known | *"The reader is the country director, who has two minutes and needs to decide on funding today."* |
| **Format** | The shape of the answer: length, structure, headings, tables | *"Use five bullet points, one line each, then one line headed 'Gaps'."* |
| **Examples** | A sample of what good looks like | *"Here is last week's update. Match its style."* |
| **Constraints** | Rules and limits: what to avoid, what to do when unsure | *"Use only information in the notes. If a figure is missing, write 'not stated'."* |

A few notes on each:

- **Role** is the least important of the six, though people often start with it. "You are an
  expert…" helps a bit, mostly by setting vocabulary and tone. It does not give the AI knowledge
  it doesn't have. A role without a clear task and context achieves little.
- **Task** should use a precise verb. "Summarize", "compare", "rewrite for", "list", "extract",
  "draft", "critique" and "translate" each lead to different outputs. "Help me with" or "do
  something about" leaves the AI to guess.
- **Context** is where most of the value is, and it's what people leave out most. The AI can't
  see your inbox, your programme documents or your manager's preferences unless you tell it.
  Who is the audience? What will they do with this? What happened last time?
- **Format** saves you editing time. If you need 150 words, say 150 words. If you need a table
  with specific columns, name the columns.
- **Examples** are so powerful they get their own section below.
- **Constraints** protect you. The most useful one for professional work is *"If the information isn't in
  what I gave you, say so. Don't make it up."* Class 2 explained why chatbots invent things.
  This constraint doesn't eliminate that risk, but it reduces it a lot.

### Weak prompt vs strong prompt

Here is the same request written two ways. Both are about a (fictional) flood in Northern
Veloria province.

!!! failure "Weak prompt"
    ```text
    Write a sitrep about the floods in Veloria.
    ```

    **What you get:** a confident, generic report that may include invented figures, invented
    organizations and a made-up timeline. The AI knows nothing about *your* floods, so it writes about
    floods in general. It doesn't know who the reader is, how long it should be or what format
    your office uses.

!!! success "Strong prompt"
    ```text
    You are a humanitarian reporting officer. [ROLE]

    Turn the field notes below into a short situation update. [TASK]

    The update is for our country director, who will read it on her phone
    before a 9:00 funding meeting. She needs to know how many people are
    affected, the top needs, and what is already being done. [CONTEXT]

    Format: a one-line headline, then these headings: Affected population /
    Priority needs / Response so far / Gaps and unknowns. Under 200 words
    in total. [FORMAT]

    Use only the information in the notes. If a figure is missing or
    unclear, write "not stated" or "unclear" — do not estimate. Flag any
    dates that could be read two ways. [CONSTRAINTS]

    Field notes:
    """
    (paste the notes here)
    """
    ```

    **What you get:** a short, structured update built from *your* information, with the gaps
    clearly marked for you to follow up.

You don't need the labels in square brackets. They are only there to show the building blocks.
Notice also the triple quotation marks (`"""`) around the pasted notes. Marking clearly where
your instructions end and your pasted material begins helps the AI keep them apart. Any clear
marker works: quotation marks, a line of dashes, or a heading such as "Field notes:".

!!! tip "Longer isn't automatically better"
    The strong prompt is longer, but its length isn't what makes it strong. Every sentence gives
    the AI something it couldn't have guessed. Padding such as long preambles, repeated
    instructions or three paragraphs of background it doesn't need can bury the important part.
    Aim for **complete**, not **long**.

## 3. Iterating: a conversation, not a vending machine

A vending machine works like this: you press a button, something drops out, and if it's wrong
you walk away. Many people use AI tools this way. They type one prompt, look at the answer,
decide "AI isn't good at this", and close the window.

A chat tool is a *conversation*. The first answer is a first draft. The real skill is often in
the second, third and fourth messages. Within a single chat, the AI can see everything said so
far, so you can build on it instead of starting again.

!!! warning "The AI only remembers this conversation"
    The AI can see the messages *in the current chat*. In most enterprise tools, a new chat
    starts from a blank page. It doesn't remember what you told it yesterday unless your tool has
    a clearly labelled memory or "project" feature switched on. If you start a new chat, give the
    context again.

Here are four follow-up moves that work.

**1. Tell it exactly what to fix.** "That's not quite right" gives the AI very little to work
with. Be specific, as you would with a colleague's draft:

```text
Good start. Three changes:
1. Cut it to half the length.
2. The reader is the donor, not our own team — remove internal acronyms.
3. Move the water situation to the top; it is the most urgent need.
```

**2. Ask it to ask you questions first.** When you're not sure what context the AI needs, let
it tell you. This is one of the most useful habits in this class:

```text
I need to write a two-page concept note for a hygiene project in
Aramu district. Before you draft anything, ask me the questions you
need answered to do this well. Ask them one at a time.
```

The questions themselves are often useful, because they show you what a good concept note needs
and what you hadn't yet thought about.

**3. Ask for options, not one answer.** The AI can produce alternatives in seconds. Take
advantage of that:

```text
Give me three different subject lines for this email: one formal, one
warm, one very short. Tell me which you would pick and why.
```

**4. Ask it to critique its own work.** A second look often catches problems:

```text
Now review your summary against the original notes. List any figure,
name or date in your summary that does not appear in the notes.
```

This last move is worth making a habit for anything that includes numbers. It isn't a
replacement for checking yourself (Class 6 covers verification in depth), but it catches a
surprising number of slips.

!!! tip "When to start a fresh chat"
    If a conversation has gone badly off course, or has become very long and the AI seems to be
    "forgetting" your early instructions, start a new chat. Paste in your improved prompt,
    written with everything you learned from the failed attempt. You're not starting over, you're
    starting with a better brief.

## 4. Showing, not telling: examples (few-shot)

Some things are hard to describe but easy to recognise: a tone of voice, a house style, the
right level of detail. "Write it in a warm but professional tone" means different things to
different people. Showing an example removes the guesswork.

Giving the AI one or more samples of what you want is called **few-shot prompting**. With no
examples it's called **zero-shot**. It works because the model is very good at continuing
patterns. Show it two examples that follow a pattern and it will follow the same pattern for the
third.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/few-shot.md"

Here is a few-shot prompt for turning dry figures into short updates for a donor newsletter:

```text
Turn each set of facts into a two-sentence donor update. Follow the
style of the examples exactly.

Facts: 300 hygiene kits distributed in Lomi village; funded by the
Northern Fund.
Update: Thanks to the Northern Fund, 300 families in Lomi village now
have soap, water containers and purification tablets. These kits are a
first line of defence against waterborne disease after the floods.

Facts: Mobile clinic reopened in Tavet; 85 patients seen in first week.
Update: The mobile clinic in Tavet is open again, and 85 people were
treated in its first week. For many families it is the only health
service within a day's walk.

Facts: Temporary classrooms set up for 240 children in Aramu town.
Update:
```

The AI will almost certainly produce two sentences, lead with the impact on people, and end with
why it matters, because that's what both examples did. You never had to explain "lead with
people, then give significance". The examples said it for you.

Tips for good examples:

- **Two or three is usually enough.** One example can be copied too literally. Several varied
  examples show the pattern rather than one instance of it.
- **Vary the content, keep the style.** If both examples are about hygiene kits, the AI may
  assume every update is about hygiene kits.
- **Your examples set the standard.** If your sample contains jargon, a typo or an unsupported
  claim, expect the AI to reproduce it.
- **Real past work makes good examples**, as long as you are allowed to share it with the tool.
  An approved, published report from last year is fine. An internal draft with personal details
  is not.

## 5. Asking the AI to think step by step

Chatbots write one word at a time, and each word is influenced by the words already written.
So if the AI jumps straight to an answer, it has had no chance to "work anything out" first.
When a task involves several steps, such as adding up figures, comparing options or checking a
list of conditions, asking the AI to reason through it step by step before answering tends to
give better results. This is called **chain-of-thought** prompting.

--8<-- "shared/sidebars/chain-of-thought.md"

The simplest version is to add *"Think this through step by step before giving your final
answer."* Even better is to spell out the steps yourself, because then you control the method:

```text
Three field teams sent the figures below for displaced households in
Aramu district. Before giving a total:
1. List each team's figure and which villages it covers.
2. Point out any village counted by more than one team.
3. Point out any figure that is a range or estimate rather than a count.
4. Only then give a total, and say how confident you are in it and why.

Team A: Aramu town, 640 households.
Team B: Lomi and Tavet, about 350–400 households.
Team C: Tavet and Dorra, 210 households (Dorra not visited, estimate
from village leader by phone).
```

A one-shot "what's the total?" might well return a neat single number. The step-by-step version
should show you that Tavet may be counted twice, that Team B gave a range, and that Dorra's
figure is second-hand. Those are exactly the things a careful reporting officer would flag.

Two cautions:

- **Visible reasoning isn't proof.** A well-organised explanation can still contain a wrong
  number. The value is that the steps make errors *easier to find*, so read them.
- **Some tools already do this.** Newer "reasoning" models think before answering on their own
  (as of 2026). Laying out the steps you want is still useful, because it makes the AI follow
  *your* method and show its working in a way you can check.

## 6. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Being vague** ("Write about the floods") | The AI fills every gap with generic guesses | Name the task, audience, purpose and format |
| **Leaving out context you think is obvious** | The AI can't see your office, your files or your history | Imagine briefing someone on their first day. What would they need to know? |
| **Asking for everything at once** (report + email + slides + translation) | Quality drops, and the AI skips or blends parts | Break the job into steps and do one at a time in the same chat |
| **Believing the first answer** | First drafts contain errors and invented details | Iterate, ask it to check itself, and verify facts and figures yourself |
| **Assuming it remembers earlier chats** | Most tools start each new chat from zero | Re-paste key context, or use your tool's approved custom instructions or project feature |
| **Shouting or pleading** ("YOU MUST!!!", "please please") | Capital letters and politeness don't unlock better answers. Clarity does | Say what you want plainly and explain *why* it matters |
| **Padding the prompt** | Important instructions get lost in long, repetitive text | Every sentence should tell the AI something it couldn't guess |
| **Not saying what to do when information is missing** | The AI tends to fill gaps with plausible guesses | Add: *"If it isn't in the text, write 'not stated'. Don't estimate."* |
| **Pasting confidential or personal data** | Information you paste may be stored, logged or seen outside your team, depending on the tool and its approval | Use only approved tools, only for data they are approved for, and remove names and identifying details |

!!! danger "Before you paste anything"
    Before you paste a document into an AI tool, ask yourself: *Is this tool approved for this
    kind of information?* Beneficiary names, medical details, staff matters, security
    information and unpublished negotiating positions need particular care. When in doubt,
    anonymise ("Village A", "Staff member 1") or don't paste it. See the [data rules on the
    About page](../about.md#data). Class 8 covers data safety in full.

A word on politeness. Saying "please" and "thank you" is harmless, and it is fine if it keeps
your writing natural. But it isn't a technique. The answer improves because of the *content* of
your request, not its manners. The same goes for capital letters, exclamation marks and threats.
If the AI keeps ignoring an instruction, the usual reason is that the instruction is unclear,
conflicts with another one, or is buried in the middle of a long prompt. Move it to the end,
state it plainly and give the reason: *"Keep it under 100 words, because it will be read aloud
in a two-minute radio slot."*

## 7. Reusable prompt templates

Once a prompt works well, don't lose it. Many of your tasks recur: the weekly update, the
meeting summary, the email declining an invitation, the translation check. A saved template
turns a good prompt into a habit and saves you rethinking the brief every time.

Here is a general template built from the six blocks. Copy it into a document you keep handy,
fill in the square brackets and delete any line you don't need.

```text
ROLE: You are [a role with relevant expertise, e.g. "an experienced
programme officer in a humanitarian organization"].

TASK: [Action verb] [what you want], e.g. "Draft a reply to the email
below, accepting the meeting and proposing two alternative times."

CONTEXT:
- This is for [who will read it] who needs to [what they will do with it].
- Background: [what the AI needs to know that it can't guess].
- The tone should be [e.g. formal / warm / neutral / urgent].

FORMAT: [length, structure, headings, bullets or table columns],
e.g. "Under 150 words, three short paragraphs."

EXAMPLES (optional): Here is an example of the style I want:
"""
[paste a good past example]
"""

CONSTRAINTS:
- Use only the information I provide. If something is missing, say
  "not stated" instead of guessing.
- Avoid [jargon / acronyms / specific words or claims].
- [Any other rule, e.g. "British English", "no bullet points"].

Before you start, ask me up to three questions if anything important
is unclear.

MATERIAL:
"""
[paste the text to work on, after checking it is safe to share]
"""
```

Some ways to get more out of templates:

- **Build a small library.** Three to five templates for your most frequent tasks is plenty.
  Name them clearly, for example "Weekly SitRep", "Donor update", "Meeting summary".
- **Share them with your team.** A shared template gives more consistent results across
  colleagues. It is also a quiet way to build in good habits such as "write 'not stated'".
- **Improve them over time.** When you have to correct the same thing twice, add a line to the
  template so you never have to correct it again.
- **Use your tool's features if they are approved.** Many tools let you save standing
  instructions (see the *system prompt* sidebar above) so your background context applies
  automatically.

## Prompt Lab

Work in pairs. Use your organization's **approved** AI chat tool. All the exercises use the
fictional field report below, from our running case study, the **SitRep Assistant**. Copy the
report once and keep it ready to paste.

!!! example "The field report (fictional)"
    ```text
    FIELD NOTE - Aramu district, Northern Veloria - sent Thurs eve
    From: K. Osei, Veloria Relief Coalition field team

    heavy rain since the weekend, Tula river burst near Kessa bridge.
    we got to Aramu town + Lomi and Tavet villages today. could NOT reach
    Dorra, road cut after the bridge, no phone signal there either.

    approx 1,200 households displaced in total (our estimate + figures
    from village chiefs). most are staying at Aramu primary school, which
    is full - maybe 600 people there. more families in the community hall
    but we didn't manage to count them.

    water is the big problem. 2 of the 3 boreholes in Lomi are flooded
    and people are drinking from the river. Tavet health post still open
    but running low on ORS, nurse reports 14 cases of acute diarrhoea
    since Monday, mostly children under 5. no deaths reported to us.

    VRC is distributing food at the school, enough stock for about 3 days.
    next truck expected 04/05 if the Kessa road is passable. urgent: water
    purification, hygiene kits, ORS, shelter materials for the hall.
    ```

Notice what's messy about it: the date is only "Thurs", the next delivery date "04/05" could be
4 May or 5 April, the number of people in the community hall is missing, and "1,200 households"
is partly an estimate. A good SitRep flags all of this instead of hiding it.

### Exercise 1 · From weak to strong

**Task:** First, send the weak prompt with the report pasted underneath. Then open a **new chat**
and send the strong version. Compare the two answers side by side.

```text
Summarize this.
```

```text
You are a humanitarian reporting officer. Turn the field note below into
a short situation update for our country director, who will read it on
her phone before a funding meeting and needs to know: how many people
are affected, the most urgent needs, and what is already being done.

Format: a one-line headline, then four headings: Affected population /
Priority needs / Response so far / Gaps and unknowns. Under 200 words.

Use only information in the note. Do not estimate missing figures. If a
date could be read two ways, say so.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field report here]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Did the weak version invent or "tidy up" anything, such as a total for the community hall or
  a definite date?
- Does the strong version put the most urgent need (water) near the top?
- Is "04/05" flagged as ambiguous, and is Dorra listed as a gap?
- Which of the six building blocks made the biggest difference, in your view?

### Exercise 2 · A table, with honest gaps

**Task:** Ask for structured output, with a fixed set of columns and a clear rule for missing
information.

```text
From the field note below, extract the key facts into a table with these
columns: Location | People or households affected | Main needs |
Response under way | Source or confidence.

Rules:
- One row per location mentioned (Aramu town, Lomi, Tavet, Dorra, plus
  one row for the community hall if it is described separately).
- If information is not in the note, write "not stated". Never estimate.
- In the last column, say whether each figure is a count, an estimate,
  or second-hand.

After the table, list every date in the note and explain any that are
ambiguous.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field report here]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Does Dorra's row say "not stated" instead of made-up figures?
- Is the community hall number left blank ("not stated") rather than guessed?
- Does it separate the 1,200 *households* from the ~600 *people*, or wrongly mix the two units?
- Would this table be easy to paste into a spreadsheet and compare with tomorrow's report?

### Exercise 3 · Matching a tone with examples

**Task:** Use few-shot prompting to turn the report into a short donor update that matches an
existing house style.

```text
Write a short update for our donors' newsletter based on the field note
below. Match the style of these two past updates exactly: length, tone
and structure.

Example 1:
"When the Sela river flooded in March, 400 families lost their homes
overnight. Within 48 hours, your support put clean water and hygiene
kits in their hands. The work is not over: 90 families are still
waiting for shelter materials."

Example 2:
"In Kopa district, a single health post serves 12,000 people. Thanks to
you, it now has a month's supply of essential medicines. Next, we aim
to train two more community health workers."

Rules: no figures that are not in the note; do not name individual
staff; do not mention exact locations of shelters.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field report here]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Does the update follow the pattern of the examples: people first, then the support, then
  what's still needed?
- Is it about the same length as the examples?
- Did it keep to the rules: no staff names ("K. Osei"), no invented figures?
- Try it again with *no examples* (zero-shot). What changes?

### Exercise 4 · Let the AI interview you

**Task:** Instead of writing a full brief, let the AI find out what it needs to know. One
partner plays the "programme officer" and answers its questions. Make up plausible fictional
answers.

```text
I need to draft a one-page funding appeal for the Aramu flood response,
based on the field note below. Before you write anything, interview me:
ask the questions you need answered to write a strong appeal, one at a
time, and wait for my answer before asking the next. When you have
enough, tell me, summarize what you learned, and then write the draft.

Field note:
"""
[paste the field report here]
"""
```

**What to look for:**

- Were the questions useful? Did any of them point out something you hadn't considered, such as
  the target donor, the amount needed or the deadline?
- Did the final draft use your answers, or drift back to generic text?
- Did it ask about the gaps in the note (Dorra, the community hall)?
- Would you use this "interview me" move in your own work? For which tasks?

**Task:** Pick a real, recurring task from your own job that is **safe to use with your approved

**What to look for:**

## Key terms

- **Prompt:** everything you type into an AI tool, including instructions, questions, pasted
  material and follow-ups.
- **Prompting (prompt engineering):** the skill of writing instructions that get useful output
  from an AI.
- **Role, task, context, format, examples, constraints:** the six building blocks of a good
  prompt.
- **Iteration:** improving an answer through follow-up messages in the same conversation.
- **Zero-shot prompting:** asking for a task with instructions only, no examples.
- **Few-shot prompting:** including a few examples of the output you want so the AI copies the
  pattern.
- **Chain of thought:** asking the AI to work through a problem in steps before answering.
- **System prompt / custom instructions:** standing instructions that apply to every message in
  a chat or project.
- **Structured output:** an answer in a fixed shape, such as a table, form or data format.
- **Prompt template:** a saved, fill-in-the-blanks prompt for a task you do often.
